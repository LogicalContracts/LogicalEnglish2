# L4 and Logical English

*Kind: integration guide · Audience: users · Status: current (2026-09-30)*

L4 is a language for writing laws and contracts as programs. The Centre for
Computational Law of Singapore Management University (SMU) built it, and the
company Legalese develops it now. An L4 program has two halves:

- **rules**, which say what follows from what: `DECIDE` a person
  `is eligible` `IF` the person is over 18 `AND` …;
- **contracts**, which say who must, may or must not do what, and by when:
  `PARTY` the seller `MUST` deliver the goods `WITHIN` 14 days `HENCE` …
  `LEST` …

Logical English (LE) reads both halves. **File ▸ Open…** (or **File ▸ Import
from Another System…**) takes an `.l4` file. The rules become an ordinary
Logical English program, with the program's own tests as scenarios. The
contracts become a program in **Logical English for LPS**, the form of Logical
English for rules about time and change, which the LPS2 editor runs. LPS
stands for Logic Production System. The translator is part of the InsurLE
extensions, so only installations that have the extensions, such as the
hosted service, offer it. The translations of L4's own examples, called
*twins*, come with every installation.

## L4 in brief: a small example

The file below, `refund.l4`, is a complete L4 program. It has one rule, one
contract and four tests.

```l4
§ `Refunds`

DECLARE Purchase HAS
    `buyer's age`         IS A NUMBER
    `days since purchase` IS A NUMBER

GIVEN p IS A Purchase
GIVETH A BOOLEAN
DECIDE p `gives a right to a refund` IF
        p's `days since purchase` <= 30
    OR  p's `buyer's age` >= 65

DECLARE Party IS ONE OF Buyer, Seller
DECLARE Action IS ONE OF `return the goods`, `refund` HAS amount IS A NUMBER

GIVETH A DEONTIC Party Action
`refund process` MEANS
    PARTY Buyer
    MAY `return the goods`
    WITHIN 30
    HENCE
        PARTY Seller
        MUST refund 100
        WITHIN 14
        LEST BREACH BY Seller BECAUSE "did not refund"

#EVAL (Purchase WITH `buyer's age` IS 40, `days since purchase` IS 10) `gives a right to a refund`
#EVAL (Purchase WITH `buyer's age` IS 40, `days since purchase` IS 45) `gives a right to a refund`
#TRACE `refund process` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY Buyer DOES `return the goods` AT 5
    PARTY Seller DOES refund 100 AT 12
#TRACE `refund process` AT 0 WITH
    PARTY Buyer DOES `return the goods` AT 5
    PARTY Seller DOES refund 100 AT 25
```

Read it from the top:

- `§` starts a section, and the words in backquotes are a name. A name in
  backquotes may be a whole phrase.
- `DECLARE Purchase HAS …` declares a **record**: a purchase has two fields,
  the buyer's age and the days since the purchase, both numbers.
- `GIVEN` names the inputs of what follows, and `GIVETH` says what kind of
  value it gives: here, yes or no (a `BOOLEAN`).
- `DECIDE … IF …` is a **rule**. A purchase gives a right to a refund if it
  is at most 30 days old, or if the buyer is 65 or older. The indentation
  groups the `OR`. The name `gives a right to a refund` comes after its
  input, `p`, so the rule reads like a sentence. L4 calls a name placed
  among its inputs *mixfix*.
- `IS ONE OF` declares a **choice**: the parties are the buyer and the
  seller, and the actions are returning the goods and a refund, which
  carries an amount.
- `` `refund process` MEANS … `` defines a **contract**, a value of L4's
  type `DEONTIC`. The buyer *may* return the goods within 30 days. `HENCE`
  says what follows when that happens: the seller *must* refund 100 within
  14 days. `LEST` says what follows when the seller does not: a breach, by
  the seller, with the reason.
- `#EVAL` asks L4 to work out a value: a test. `#TRACE` runs the contract
  against events at given days: the first trace ends fulfilled; in the
  second the refund comes on day 25, after the deadline of day 19, and the
  contract ends breached by the seller.

Opened in this editor, the rule becomes Logical English:

```le
the functions are:
    the age of the buyer of *a purchase* is *a number*.
    the days since purchase of *a purchase* is *a number*.

the templates are:
    *a purchase* gives a right to a refund.

the knowledge base refund includes:

a purchase gives a right to a refund if
    the days since purchase of the purchase is a number N
    and N <= 30
    or the age of the buyer of the purchase is a number M
        and M >= 65.

scenario line_27 is, as stated in the source program at line 27:
    the age of the buyer of purchase_1 is 40.
    the days since purchase of purchase_1 is 10.
    line_27 expects answers ["purchase_1 gives a right to a refund"].
```

and the contract becomes a Logical English for LPS program,
`refund_contracts.le`, in which each step of the contract is a fluent:

```le
when a party does return the goods on day a day
    and refund process permits the party to return the goods by day a due day as its first obligation
    and the day <= the due day
    and a second due day = day + 14
then refund process obliges seller to refund with amount 100 by day the second due day as its second obligation
    and it is not the case that refund process permits the party to return the goods by day the due day as its first obligation.
```

Both programs pass L4's own answers: the two `#EVAL`s as scenarios, and the
two `#TRACE`s as runs in the LPS2 editor that end as L4's do.

## At a glance

| Direction | Where (menu item) | Files | What you get | Checked against |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L4 → Logical English | **File ▸ Open…**, **File ▸ Import from Another System…** | `.l4` | the rules as Logical English, the file's `#EVAL` and `#ASSERT` tests as scenarios, a ledger; the contracts as a Logical English for LPS program written beside it | L4's own evaluator: 621 of 621 answers over 62 twins |
| L4 → Logical English for LPS | the same menu items in the LPS2 editor | `.l4` | the contracts: each obligation a fluent, each `#TRACE` a scenario; the rules written beside it | L4's own evaluator: 87 of the 95 traces of L4's examples end where L4's do, and none differ |

There is no way back yet: the editor does not write L4.

## How to use it

### Opening an L4 file

Choose **File ▸ Open…** and pick the `.l4` file. What opens depends on the
editor:

- **In this editor**, the rules open. A file that also has contracts gets a
  second program written beside the first, `<name>_contracts.le`, and the
  note under the menu bar names it. Open the same L4 file in the LPS2 editor
  to run the contracts.
- **In the LPS2 editor**, the contracts open, and the rules are written
  beside them. A file with no contract opens as its rules in both editors.

The note under the menu bar gives the counts of the translator's ledger: how
many parts of the file were translated exactly (*encoded*), how many with a
wording the translator had to invent (*approximated*), and how many could not
be translated (*residue*). A residue is never dropped. It stays in the
program as a comment block that starts with the word TODO, with the L4 text
and the reason, so that you or the Contract Assistant can finish it.
**File ▸ Show the Original** shows the L4 file itself, kept under `sources/`
beside the program.

### L4's examples

The twins of L4's own examples are among the examples, in
`examples/migration/l4/`: the foundation course, the legal examples (the
British Nationality Act, an imaginary alcohol act, anti-social behaviour
notices, a chief executive's performance award, New York environmental review,
the promissory note), the regulatory course, the reference examples and the
Jersey charities law. Each folder keeps its L4 source under `sources/`, with
L4's licence (Apache 2.0).

A folder whose file has contracts also holds a **history view**,
`<name>_history.le` ([below](#the-history-view)). The contract twins that run
in time are in the LPS2 editor's examples, under the same folder name.

### Where the expected answers come from

Every expected answer of a twin's scenarios is the answer **L4's own
evaluator** gives on the same file. The evaluator is L4's authors' own
program, compiled to WebAssembly (a form that runs in a browser or under
Node.js), and it runs beside the translator.

- An `#EVAL` or `#ASSERT` line becomes a query, and a scenario that expects
  the evaluator's answer.
- A `#TRACE` becomes a scenario with the trace's events at their days. L4's
  result, written above the scenario, says whether the contract ended
  fulfilled, ended breached, or what remains open.
- Many legal examples have no tests, because their facts are declared with
  `ASSUME` and the evaluator cannot run them. For such a file the translator
  writes the file again with each assumed fact given a value, and asks the
  evaluator for the answers. When a file has few enough yes-or-no facts, it
  tries every combination: 152 answers for the alcohol act, 128 for the
  anti-social behaviour notices.

When you open your own file on a server without the evaluator, the twin has
its scenarios but no expected answers, and the note says so.

## How L4's rules map to Logical English

L4 is a programming language in which every definition is a function. The
translator turns each function into a sentence pattern, a **template**, and
the definition into rules.

| L4 | Logical English |
|---|---|
| `DECIDE x IF …` (a yes-or-no question) | a rule that concludes the template of `x` |
| `x MEANS …` (a value) | a template ending `… is *a value*`, declared under `the functions are:`, and a rule that gives the value |
| the name of a function | the template's wording: the `@nlg` annotation when there is one; else a mixfix name with its inputs in place (`` p `is eligible for` q `` → `*a person* is eligible for *a program*`); else the name as a phrase; else the name with its inputs added at the end, which the ledger marks for review |
| `DECLARE Person HAS age IS A NUMBER, …` (a record) | one template per field, `the age of *a person* is *a number*`; a record the tests build becomes a named individual with one fact per field |
| `DECLARE Colour IS ONE OF Red, Green` (a choice) | named individuals |
| `ASSUME` a fact | a template marked `; scenario element`, which the scenarios state |
| `IF … THEN … ELSE`, `BRANCH`, `CONSIDER … WHEN …` | an `otherwise` cascade (reference §17.2) |
| `WHERE` and `LET` (definitions local to a function) | a template of their own, with the enclosing function's inputs as places |
| `AND`, `OR`, `NOT`, arithmetic, comparisons, `MODULO` | `and`, `or`, `it is not the case that`, the same arithmetic, `mod` |
| a function that calls itself | a rule that uses itself |
| `all` and `any` over a list | `for all cases in which …` and an ordinary condition |
| `sum`, `length` of a list | `sum of each … such that`, `count of each … such that` |
| `map` and `filter` kept as a list | `the list of each … such that` (reference §5) |
| dates (`daydate`) | the library `lib/l4_daydate.le`, which counts days as L4 does |
| `§` sections | `section … is:` |
| `@ref`, `@desc` | a citation of the source, a comment |

What has no Logical English form stays as residue: text conversions, web
calls, a function passed to another function as a value, a record built
inside a rule.

## How L4's contracts map to Logical English for LPS

The LPS2 documentation has the full account
([L4 and LPS](https://lps2.logicalcontracts.com/docs/user/integrations/l4)).
In short: each obligation, permission or prohibition is a **fluent**, a fact
that holds for a while, such as `the sale contract obliges the seller to
deliver the goods by day 14 as its first obligation`. Each action of L4's
traces is an **event** that carries its day. A law says what meeting the
obligation starts next (L4's `HENCE`), and another says what a missed deadline
starts (L4's `LEST`).

## The history view

A contract can be read in two ways. The LPS2 editor runs it **forward**: the
days pass, events happen, deadlines are met or missed, and the timeline shows
it. The **history view** reads it **backward**: the events have all
happened, and the question is where the contract stands. That is the
question a lawyer is usually asked: which obligations were met, which were
missed, who is in breach, and what is still due on the last day.

The L4 translator writes the history view itself, with no one editing it.
It writes it from the same translation of the contract as the LPS twin: the
same obligations, in the same words. For each obligation the history view
has:

- a rule for the day it **starts**, and its deadline: at the contract's start
  for the first obligation, and for the next one, the day the one before was
  met (L4's `HENCE`) or missed (L4's `LEST`);
- a rule for the day it is **met**: the first event that matches the action
  on or before the deadline;
- a rule for the day it is **missed**: the first event after the deadline,
  since L4 notices a missed deadline only at the next event;
- a rule that says it is **still open** at the end of the history.

Each of the L4 file's `#TRACE`s becomes a scenario that lists the events, and
each scenario expects what the LPS twin's run of the same trace ended with.
That run was itself checked against L4's evaluator first, so the expected
answers are L4's. Two traces of L4's examples keep no expectation, and say
why in the file:

- the wedding of the foundation course has two events on the same day, which
  a history read by days cannot put in order;
- the promissory note's history of twelve payments takes longer than the test
  runner allows.

The four rules are the same for every obligation, so they could have been a
library, `lib/deontic_history.le`, included by every history view. The
translator writes them out for each obligation instead, because the library
was much slower. The library needs one sentence, `the obligation that *a
sentence* starts on *a date*`, for every kind of obligation. One kind of
obligation starts when another is missed, which is a negation, so the same
sentence then depends on its own negation. Logicians call such a program
*unstratified*, and the system warns about it. Logical English can still run
it, but it cannot work out the answers kind by kind, and each question makes
it work out the whole history again. With one sentence for each obligation,
the program is stratified. On the promissory note's twelve payments, the
written-out rules take about 9 seconds, and the library had not finished
after 60.

## What L4 does that surprises readers

L4's evaluator, not its documentation, decides what an L4 program means. The
twins reproduce the evaluator of L4's repository at commit `5e082cf` (29
September 2026; a later L4 may differ), including five behaviours that
readers of the L4 text may not expect:

- **A missed deadline is noticed only when something next happens.** A
  contract whose deadline passed in silence is still open until the next
  event. That event then starts what the `LEST` branch says, and the event
  itself does not count towards the new obligation.
- **Each deadline counts from the moment its obligation started.** `WITHIN 30`
  after a payment means 30 days after that payment, not after the start of the
  contract. In the promissory note, whose instalments are computed as days
  after the start of the loan, the deadlines therefore drift further with
  every payment.
- **Paying the promissory note's penalty ends the whole note.** The penalty
  obligation's `HENCE` is `FULFILLED`, so the instalments that remain are no
  longer owed.
- **A name inside an action binds rather than compares.** In `MUST pay
  amount`, where `amount` is an input of the contract, any payment meets the
  obligation, and `amount` takes the value paid. Only `EXACTLY` compares the
  action with the input's value.
- **A permission that is not used still leads on.** In the example above, if
  the buyer never returns the goods, L4's evaluator still starts the
  seller's obligation to refund once the 30 days have passed and something
  next happens. L4's documentation says instead that an unused permission
  ends the contract as fulfilled. The twins follow the evaluator, which
  gives L4's own answers.

## What is not supported yet

The translator keeps what it cannot translate in the program, as residue:
a comment block starting with TODO, with the L4 text and the reason. Over
the rules of L4's examples, 116 of about 1,780 parts of the files are
residue. (The rules programs mark 43 more parts as residue: the contracts,
which the contracts program translates.) The residue falls into these
groups:

- **Values Logical English has no form for:** text functions (joining texts,
  turning a number into text), conversions, web calls (`FETCH`, `POST`) and
  the reading and writing of JSON, a data format of the web.
- **Records and alternatives built inside a rule.** A rule that builds a
  new record (`Money WITH Currency IS "USD", Value IS …`) or an `EITHER`
  value has no Logical English form. Records that the tests build are
  translated, as named individuals. For the same reason, a test whose answer
  is a record, such as the promissory note's amounts, keeps no expected
  answer.
- **Functions passed as values** to other functions, other than the small
  functions written in place for `all`, `any`, `map` and `filter`.
- **Lists built one element at a time**, with `FOLLOWED BY`, and `TRUNC` with
  a number of decimal places.
- **Contracts.** 8 of the 95 traces of L4's examples are not translated. 7
  are in the chief executive's performance award, which takes lists apart
  with `FOLLOWED BY` and reads a field of a value known only when the
  contract runs. The eighth is in the New York environmental review, which
  applies a function to fewer inputs than it takes. Each stays as a scenario
  with its reason. `RAND` and `ROR` are translated, but not one inside
  another.
- **Programs split over several files**, which must be opened file by file.
- **The way back.** The editor does not write L4.
- **L4's newest syntax.** The translator was built on L4 as of 29 September
  2026.

## Traps

- **Words Logical English keeps for itself are changed.** A template cannot
  contain the words LE uses for its own sentences (`if`, `and`, `or`,
  `otherwise`, `unless` …). The translator writes `is an individual aged 16
  else over` for L4's `` `is an individual aged 16 or over` ``, and the
  ledger marks each such wording for review.
- **Two definitions whose sentences could be read as each other** are told
  apart with a word such as `given` or `variant two`. The ledger records
  which.
- **A program split over several L4 files** must be opened file by file: L4's
  evaluator, which gives the expected answers, can be given one file only.
- **L4 moves fast.** The translator was built on L4's repository as it stood
  on 29 September 2026. Older files in syntax L4 no longer accepts are read as
  far as possible, and what cannot be read is residue.
